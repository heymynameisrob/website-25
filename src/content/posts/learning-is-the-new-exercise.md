Every job I've worked at, there was always somebody who helped me take a big leap in learning. It was typically an engineer who showed me a pattern I was using incorrectly, helped me solve a problem I thought unsolveable, or pushed me to improve sloppy parts of my code.

Typically this came in the form of PR comments, crits, or casual conversations. I would then goto this person continually to ask questions, get advice, or help me solve problems.

This learning came for free. It was part of the task of solving the problem I was working and doing my job correctly. Little did we know how good we had it

I, like many developers, use AI on a daily basis. What started with autocomplete or basic research has now become a fully agentic development process where I'm typing less than 10% of the code I write. I still consider my workflow pretty primitive by modern standards. Yet, it's very rare I'm not steering coding agents to fix issues or give me functionality I've requested.

It's unarguable it's made me more productive. The speed at which I can crank out passable code with it is amazing. On average, I don't feel I need to re-write much of it either. Some proper scoping or scaffolding with types or a technical document is typically enough for my agent to pull patterns from my refined library of skills and existing patterns in mature repos.

Recently, I spent a few months going all in. I decided to try letting AI do 99% of the work, where I was it's manager giving it feedback on what I wanted. Whilst being "more productive", I realised that what I was learning literaly nothing. Not only that, but my capacity for learning generally was atrophied.

Even if I had to solve a simple problem, coding agents would do it for me. I'd return back to the code it'd written a week later and be completely lost. My original approach abandoned in the persuit of achieving the goal I'd given it. My understanding was either out of date or non-existant. This meant nothing I did compounded. I was merely a QA assistant giving the green light to something I didn't have a connection with.

There is some argument to say 'So what?'. If agents can write and understand the code, just tell it what you want. You're the director. But even though this feels like a hollow existance, representing the opposite of why I love what I do so much - this doesn't make sense for your career or your products.

Learning and solving problems doesn't just help you get from A to B - it helps you build the muscels to do it faster.

It's ironic that we have one of the best learning tools available to us with LLMs yet many of us care little to how it came up with its answer to our question. Or how it knows it's correct. Or how it can teach us to never need to ask this question again. It's merely the cognitive equivilant of the washing machine - taking a task we don't want to do and doing it for us.

Modern humans deliberately exercise because our bodies are built to track animals and travel across the savana. We no longer need to do this economically, but we do phsyically. So we exercise. The concept of walking for 30 minutes or running on a treadmill to Bronze age man would be absurd. Why would you expend energy for no reason?

I think learning is becoming the same. I don't think we should abandon the promising future of AI giving us cognitive super-powers. I also am open, yet sketpical, to the idea of AI doing more and better thinking than us. But what we do need to continue doing is learning. We must learn for it's own sake. To keep our minds healthy and our lives rich and full.
